The Reserve Bank of Australia raised its benchmark cash rate for the fifth time this year, pushing borrowing costs to their highest level since the 2008 global financial crisis. The decision was driven by inflation that continues to run above the central bank&\#x27;s target. The move directly raises repayments for millions of Australian households on variable-rate mortgages and tightens conditions for businesses, weighing on Australian equities, bonds and the dollar. It also reinforces the broader picture of central banks keeping policy restrictive for longer rather than pivoting quickly to cuts. The RBA moves in increments of 25 basis points, and the cash rate — the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans — feeds through to mortgage, business and savings rates across the economy. The bank aims to keep annual consumer price inflation between 2 and 3 per cent over the medium term, and sets policy at board meetings held eight times a year.

rss · FT Markets · Nov 3, 06:40

**Background**: Australia&\#x27;s central bank controls the cash rate, the overnight lending rate between banks, which in turn influences the interest rates households and businesses actually pay or earn. Like many central banks, the RBA operates an inflation-targeting framework: when inflation runs hot, it raises rates to cool demand, and when the economy weakens, it cuts. Australia&\#x27;s inflation target is defined as annual consumer price inflation of 2 to 3 per cent, so a prolonged overshoot is the trigger for the tightening cycle described here.

**Background**: The FX deposit reserve ratio is the share of foreign-currency deposits that banks must hold as reserves at the central bank, the foreign-currency counterpart of the familiar RMB reserve requirement ratio; raising it reduces the foreign exchange banks can lend or convert, tightening FX liquidity.
