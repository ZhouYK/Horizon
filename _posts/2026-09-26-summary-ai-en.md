## [U.S., Russia Stripped Human Oversight From Global AI Weapons Pact](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMirgFBVV95cUxPblNLMmd2Y0t3cS1KN01EbFRNdzc0cHpWT193NnJHNXN1SndBTjdISVdYVGlIbnN0NHRROWJYdDlaeU1VelUwc2hwZ1c5d3dZcjhBQnVNMVF3UVFRd0hDOGkwTTVMSkFBTHM3VG9sU2NJakQ3SGNuMmFUanAzV0tGbXhNOFl6elFaOXZxNEwweUpmSXF5cDlfbmQ2Y0xjMWUxRkNHSW5iUEJNTnR6MFE?oc=5) ⭐️ 7.0/10

According to a Washington Post report, the United States and Russia removed or weakened provisions requiring human oversight from a global agreement on AI-powered weapons. The report details how the two countries stripped language intended to keep humans in control of such weapons. This matters because the change could undermine international efforts to establish meaningful human control over lethal autonomous weapons, weakening a key norm before binding rules exist. It may also affect how militaries develop and deploy AI-enabled weapons and how accountability is handled under international humanitarian law. The pact in question is a political commitment rather than a legally binding treaty, so removing oversight language weakens normative pressure without creating direct legal obligations. The debate often centers on “meaningful human control” and accountability when AI-enabled weapons use force.

google\_news · The Washington Post · Sep 26, 16:00

**Background**: Lethal autonomous weapons systems \(LAWS\) are weapons that can select and engage targets without human intervention, and since 2016 they have been discussed at the UN through a Group of Governmental Experts under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons. The U.S. also led a Political Declaration on Responsible Military Use of AI and Autonomy, a non-binding norms initiative announced at the REAIM summit that covers autonomous weapons and weapons decision-making; as of late 2024 it had 58 signatories. Human oversight, often framed as “meaningful human control,” is central to these debates because it addresses who is accountable when AI-enabled weapons use force.

## [Washington Post: Government Agencies Use AI to Decide Public Records Releases](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMixwFBVV95cUxOeUdRMmdxcXBOTHpHMzVmYXBtUUN3SUYtSWdxV0ZxRHUzZmdzQlJLZHIweGRsZmdWSkJ0ZVdsbWMyVnpWMFNqZlRQX25kUjFGOGw0cFlnXy1Ra2l5YmdpOWZ0dlFMZHNKUE0xNDJ6dGVBbXcyNGdxUUwyWTJRbW9iYWV5TTkxdDZwdGY3U1JfOE1tVmlyanc0U1dBNjhEY2wydmk2WXlEOXZFU2F2NWE1LXRHdUhZVlA0bWlXYTFYaGU5WWxidVRN?oc=5) ⭐️ 6.0/10

A Washington Post analysis reports that government agencies are enlisting artificial intelligence to help decide which public records are released to the public. The piece examines how AI tools are being folded into the records-review process — the stage at which officials judge whether requested documents can be disclosed or must be withheld or redacted. If AI is used to filter or withhold records, it could quietly reshape the public&\#x27;s access to government information, since errors or bias in the model may go unnoticed by requesters. The debate sits at the intersection of two major trends: rapid AI adoption across government operations and long-standing pressure on freedom-of-information systems. Records review is typically a labor-intensive task in which staff apply statutory exemptions covering areas such as personal privacy, national security and law enforcement, and AI can speed up flagging, de-duplication and redaction. The key caveat raised by such reporting is accountability: if a model recommends withholding a document, requesters and journalists may have little visibility into how that decision was made.

google\_news · The Washington Post · Sep 26, 17:00

**Background**: In the United States, the Freedom of Information Act \(FOIA\), enacted in 1966, gives the public the right to request records from federal agencies, which generally must respond within a set number of working days and may only withhold material under specific exemptions. In practice, agencies face large backlogs and enormous document volumes, which is why automation — and more recently AI — has become attractive to records officers. Public records law at the state and local level works similarly, and disputes over delays, redactions and denials are a recurring feature of transparency journalism.
