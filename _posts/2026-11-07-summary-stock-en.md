## [China&\#x27;s Financial Law second draft adds consumer protection and foreign access rules](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-11-07/102502284.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

On November 6, 2026, China&\#x27;s Financial Law \(Draft\) second review version was published for public comment, adding financial consumer protection provisions and targeted rules for internet-based financial marketing. It also introduces a market-access clause requiring foreign institutions to obtain administrative approval before providing financial products and services within China. As the first unified &quot;1+X&quot; framework law for China&\#x27;s financial sector, the draft would turn cross-border provision of financial products by foreign firms from a regulatory-cooperation matter into an administrative licensing item, materially raising compliance requirements for banks, brokers, insurers, fintech platforms and foreign financial institutions. Because it is still a consultative draft, immediate market impact is limited, but it signals a broad regulatory shift. The market-access clause is the most material addition, converting what was previously handled through cross-border regulatory cooperation into an approval requirement. The draft also strengthens financial consumer and investor protection and adds specific rules on internet marketing, though it remains a consultative draft subject to further revision.

**Background**: The Financial Law is described as the first overarching law named after &quot;finance&quot; globally, positioned as the &quot;1&quot; in China&\#x27;s &quot;1+X&quot; financial legal framework that guides and coordinates sector-specific laws. The 20th CPC Central Committee&\#x27;s Third Plenum in July 2024 explicitly called for enacting the law; a first draft was released for public comment in March 2026, and the first review took place at the NPC Standing Committee in June 2026. The second review occurred in early November 2026, with the revised draft published for comment on November 6.

## [Former TCM Administration Head Yu Yanhong Turns Herself In](https://china.caixin.com/2026-11-06/102501634.html) ⭐️ 6.0/10

On November 6, 2026, the Central Commission for Discipline Inspection and the National Supervisory Commission announced that Yu Yanhong, former Party group member of the National Health Commission and former Party secretary and director of the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine, had voluntarily surrendered and is under disciplinary review and supervisory investigation for suspected serious violations of discipline and law. The announcement came just two days after Yu, 64, was stripped of her seat on the National Committee of the Chinese People&\#x27;s Political Consultative Conference, making her the 58th centrally managed official publicly reported as fallen in 2026. Yu&\#x27;s case makes the National Administration of Traditional Chinese Medicine the rare regulator where two consecutive directors have been investigated, reinforcing that China&\#x27;s anti-corruption drive is still intensifying across the healthcare and pharmaceutical sectors. For investors and companies in traditional Chinese medicine, hospital procurement, and health policy, it is a governance and policy-risk signal rather than a direct hit to any single company&\#x27;s earnings. The Caixin report emphasizes that Yu had spent her career in the medical and pharmaceutical field and that two successive heads of the TCM administration have now been investigated, hence the headline phrase “前腐后继” \(corrupt one after another\). Her case is described as “主动投案” \(voluntary surrender\) rather than being announced after an investigation had formally begun, a distinction that under CCDI rules can be treated as a mitigating factor for lenient handling.

**Background**: Yu is a “centrally managed official” \(中管干部\), meaning her appointment and oversight sit with the Central Organization Department rather than a provincial or ministerial body, which is why her case is announced by the CCDI itself. ## [China&\#x27;s central bank adds 26 tonnes of gold in October, extending buying streak to 24 months](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-11-07/102502280.html) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Data released by the State Administration of Foreign Exchange \(SAFE\) on November 7, 2026 showed that China&\#x27;s official gold reserves reached 78.30 million ounces at the end of October, up 830,000 ounces \(about 26 tonnes\) from September. This is the 24th consecutive month of central-bank gold purchases, and the single-month increase is second only to the 840,000-ounce jump in September 2023. The continued buying shows that the People&\#x27;s Bank of China kept accumulating gold even as prices pulled back in October, reinforcing a structural trend of reserve diversification away from dollar-denominated assets that has supported global gold demand and prices. Sustained official-sector purchases give the gold market a stable, price-insensitive source of demand and are closely watched by investors as a signal of central-bank attitudes toward currency and reserve risk. Over the current 24-month streak the central bank has added a cumulative 5.5 million ounces, roughly 171 tonnes — equivalent to more than 13,000 standard delivery gold bars. That total is still only about one-third of the roughly 102 tonnes... more precisely, it remains well below the scale of the previous buying cycle from November 2021 to April 2024.

rss · 财新网 · Nov 7, 12:14

**Background**: SAFE publishes China&\#x27;s official reserve assets monthly, including the gold held by the central bank, which is reported in troy ounces rather than tonnes \(one tonne is roughly 32,151 troy ounces\). Central banks buy gold as a reserve asset that is not tied to any single country&\#x27;s credit or monetary policy, and official-sector purchases have become a major driver of global gold demand in recent years. In the physical market, a &quot;standard delivery gold bar&quot; typically weighs about 12.5 kilograms \(roughly 400 troy ounces\) and is the form banks use for wholesale settlement, so reserve changes are often described in bar equivalents.
