## [Tanker Rates Hit Record $1.2mn a Day as Iran War Disrupts Shipping](https://www.ft.com/content/f95db340-8c8f-435f-957d-4623255ac29e?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Oil tanker costs have hit a record $1.2 million per day as the Iran war disrupts shipping, with a shortage of the largest supertankers more than doubling freight rates on key Middle East–China routes since late August. The spike reflects a scramble for very large crude carriers as longer, riskier voyages tie up available tonnage. Shipping is the backbone of the global oil trade, so record freight costs feed directly into crude prices, refinery margins and eventually consumer fuel and goods prices worldwide. With a substantial share of seaborne crude transiting the Strait of Hormuz, sustained disruption threatens to rekindle global inflation and reshapes the economics of shipping equities and energy supply chains. The costs apply to very large crude carriers \(VLCCs\), each capable of hauling roughly 2 million barrels of crude, and freight rates in this market are conventionally quoted in Worldscale points rather than plain dollars per tonne. The disruption stems less from a physical shortage of ships than from longer rerouting and elevated war-risk premiums, which absorb tonnage and push effective rates far above normal levels.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 23, 04:00

**Background**: A VLCC \(Very Large Crude Carrier\) is the largest class of oil tanker, designed to move unrefined crude in bulk from extraction sites to refineries, and a single vessel can carry enough oil to fill millions of barrels of storage. Tanker rates are typically quoted on the Worldscale system, a standardized reference index rather than an absolute price, so a move from WS 100 to WS 280 does not simply mean a 2.8x increase in dollars. Much of the world&\#x27;s seaborne oil passes through the Strait of Hormuz, a narrow chokepoint between the Persian Gulf and open ocean; even a temporary inability to transit it can create major supply delays and sharply raise shipping costs.

Treasury yields are the benchmark &\#x27;risk-free&\#x27; rate for global asset pricing, so a rapid rise pushes up borrowing costs for mortgages and corporate debt while pressuring equity valuations, especially in rate-sensitive sectors. Treasury yields are the returns investors earn on US government debt, and they move inversely to bond prices; because they anchor the global &\#x27;risk-free&\#x27; rate, their swings ripple through mortgages, corporate borrowing and equity valuations.
