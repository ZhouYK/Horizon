## [Report: Up to 20% of New gTLD Domains Used for Scams](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/the-purpose-of-dns-is-to-spread-scams/) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Terence Eden&\#x27;s blog post highlights an Interisle report estimating that of 85 million new gTLD domain registrations in 2025, 8.5 million were blocklisted by May 2025. The report suggests a 10% abuse rate is the floor, with the true figure likely closer to 20% — meaning one in five new gTLD domains may be scams. This matters because it highlights that the domain name system is a major vector for cybercrime, directly challenging the assumption that most internet abuse happens elsewhere. It pressures ICANN and the broader domain industry to take stronger action against domain abuse, and helps security professionals quantify the risk of newly registered domains. The Interisle report focuses on generic top-level domains \(gTLDs\), which include legacy extensions like .com and .org as well as newer ones. Terence Eden notes that ICANN has apparently been discussing this problem for years, suggesting the issue is well known yet unresolved.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 14:40

**Background**: A generic top-level domain \(gTLD\) is a type of internet domain extension maintained by IANA, such as .com, .org, or newer ones like .xyz. DNS \(Domain Name System\) translates human-friendly domain names into IP addresses, and cybercriminals often register domains to host phishing sites, malware, or scam operations before taking them down. Domain blocklists are real-time databases that track domains known to send spam or malware, so being added to a blocklist is a strong indicator of malicious use.

## [Why Rewriting Legacy Software From Scratch Usually Fails](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/6/theres-no-limit-to-how-bad-code-can-get/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Simon Willison published a commentary on Lobste.rs arguing that rewriting a legacy system from scratch rarely works. He contends that the old system remains a moving target and developer incentives align against the rewrite, often leaving two systems in production. Technical debt and rewriting decisions affect nearly every software engineering organization. Simon&\#x27;s analysis offers a realistic counterpoint to the common &\#x27;greenfield rewrite&\#x27; temptation, helping engineers and leaders evaluate incremental improvement over risky rewrites. Willison recommends shoring up the old system with automated tests and targeted refactors instead of a full rewrite. He also references Will Larson&\#x27;s essay &\#x27;Migrations: the sole scalable fix to tech debt&\#x27; as a responsible approach to large-scale system changes.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 6, 09:08

**Background**: Technical debt refers to the implied cost of additional rework caused by choosing an easy solution now instead of a better approach that would take longer. A &\#x27;greenfield rewrite&\#x27; means rebuilding a system from scratch, while &\#x27;legacy system&\#x27; refers to an older, often critical system still running core business functions. Rewrites are tempting because they promise a clean slate, but they fail when the original system&\#x27;s full behavior is poorly understood and it must keep evolving during the rewrite process.

## [Meta FAIR Introduces RPMs to Rank ML Experiments Before Spending GPU Hours](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMi4wFBVV95cUxPYXZzVU94NzFvWkZNZmNTLWYxNjFaRmJCR0g2emRYdUxTeTdCcFhOMWh3M0pIcFZRRG12Zi1WNFp3Y0EwRkxnM1h3VjY2TFlUSmZMa2ctd3hOaG44RHFFZE5sSVFOYkFLQW5YWHptaHp1cXVLZjlMSl9LTVA3emFtaVdsb204a25kandyY3R5NndfTWRNYkZ3VHhialY1NDZvcU5xNGxDYXhPQ3MtdGRyal8yN1A5OWpTNGlxRjZyLWRHMEFkVUxXeFgtWmh4eGdrLWZkSmhnaFJhb3poWEFnZTYyRdIB4wFBVV95cUxPYXZzVU94NzFvWkZNZmNTLWYxNjFaRmJCR0g2emRYdUxTeTdCcFhOMWh3M0pIcFZRRG12Zi1WNFp3Y0EwRkxnM1h3VjY2TFlUSmZMa2ctd3hOaG44RHFFZE5sSVFOYkFLQW5YWHptaHp1cXVLZjlMSl9LTVA3emFtaVdsb204a25kandyY3R5NndfTWRNYkZ3VHhialY1NDZvcU5xNGxDYXhPQ3MtdGRyal8yN1A5OWpTNGlxRjZyLWRHMEFkVUxXeFgtWmh4eGdrLWZkSmhnaFJhb3poWEFnZTYyRQ?oc=5) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Meta FAIR, together with the University of Oxford and University College London, introduced AI Research Preference Models \(RPMs\), which rank machine-learning experiments by likely success before GPU resources are committed. The method, outlined in an arXiv preprint, predicts which candidate solution is most promising without running all proposed experiments. This work attacks the high and often wasted GPU cost of exploratory machine-learning research, where many experiments fail. If RPM predictions are reliable, researchers and labs can spend their compute budget on the most promising ideas, potentially speeding up AI discovery and reducing energy consumption. According to the arXiv description, RPMs are built from frozen pretrained language models and come in two variants: an inference-only model that reasons over candidate plans, code, and previously executed solutions, and an agentic model that assists a research agent in searching over experiments. The work was done in collaboration with academic institutions rather than inside Meta alone.

google\_news · MarkTechPost · Sep 6, 20:25

**Background**: Machine learning research often requires running many training jobs or fine-tuning attempts, each consuming large amounts of GPU compute, to identify configurations that work. A research engineer may not know in advance which idea will yield the best results, so teams typically run multiple experiments in parallel and discard most outcomes. RPMs are designed as an early-stage filter: a language model evaluates the relative promise of candidate solutions to avoid wasting expensive compute on low-probability trials.
