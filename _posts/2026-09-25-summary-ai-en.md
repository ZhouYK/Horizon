## [John Gruber Warns Meta&\#x27;s Muse Is Powerful and Misunderstood](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Sep/25/john-gruber/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

In a September 25, 2026 Daring Fireball post quoted by Simon Willison, John Gruber argued that Meta&\#x27;s Muse — described as the first consumer-accessible agentic AI system — is simultaneously technically groundbreaking and dangerously powerful. He noted that each user gets an entire persistent Linux VM running in Meta&\#x27;s cloud, yet the product is packaged as an easy-to-install, cute-mascot-style app, and he doubts consumers understand what they are getting into. Muse represents the first time an autonomous, tool-using AI agent has been shipped to mainstream consumers rather than developers, which shifts the safety debate from research labs to ordinary households. If users treat an agent with persistent cloud compute and local machine access as a harmless chatbot, the potential for data loss, account compromise, or destructive actions grows sharply — a concern that will shape how regulators and platforms handle consumer agentic AI. The core technical novelty Gruber highlights is the per-user persistent Linux VM hosted in Meta&\#x27;s cloud, meaning the agent retains state, files, and context across sessions rather than resetting each time. Gruber&\#x27;s analogy is a power saw: buyers of a finger-severing tool instinctively know the risk, but Muse&\#x27;s friendly mascot packaging gives no such signal, particularly when it runs on the user&\#x27;s own Mac.

rss · Simon Willison · Sep 25, 17:22

**Background**: Agentic AI refers to systems that pursue a goal autonomously over multiple steps — planning, calling tools, and acting with limited per-step human approval — as opposed to single-turn chatbots that merely answer a prompt. Meta launched Muse in September 2026 as a US-only personal AI agent that claims to actually do the work rather than just answer questions, and observers noted it was heavily inspired by the OpenClaw project. Consumer agents of this kind raise new questions because they need broad access to a user&\#x27;s files, accounts, and devices in order to be useful at all.

## [Judges Warn AI-Generated &\#x27;Lawslop&\#x27; Filings Threaten Courts&\#x27; Ability to Function](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMipgFBVV95cUxNREVPNGpVZDhFWXpoOTV6T3UzbXVpaW9mNURHNG11Vk12ZVdIazRXdWk0aXZtdTVnc3FXN1JVVUxzaXlfTlpSaG5aY1NOeG9zYlJpSW9EVEE1WFc4SHJid0NwMTlUTXNlYlVUWkxxclNIN0ktMHY2YnZHSjJWVkJoOFlKZC1fYnA0OU15czlkZGV6ZlVvWV8yX3oxM0pqQ2FDakhiLS1n?oc=5) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Judges are publicly warning that AI-generated legal filings — dubbed &quot;lawsplop&quot; — have become so pervasive and so low in quality that they now threaten courts&\#x27; ability to function. The warnings, reported by Futurism, describe a flood of machine-written motions, briefs and arguments arriving in courtrooms faster than judges and clerks can meaningfully process them. This is one of the clearest real-world examples yet of generative AI&\#x27;s downstream costs: the technology shifts work onto the institutions that must review it, and the burden lands on courts, clerks and opposing parties rather than on the person who generated the text. It signals that AI adoption is already forcing procedural changes in a legacy, high-stakes industry where errors have direct legal and financial consequences. Much of the problem stems from AI hallucinations — chatbots confidently fabricating case citations, statutes and quotations that do not exist — which lawyers then file without verification, exposing them to sanctions. Because courts operate on strict deadlines, filing formats and docket limits, a surge of unusable submissions consumes judicial time that would otherwise go to genuine disputes.

google\_news · Futurism · Sep 25, 18:51

**Background**: Generative AI tools such as ChatGPT can produce fluent, plausible-looking legal prose, but they do not reliably retrieve real case law, so they sometimes invent citations entirely — a failure mode known as hallucination. The term &quot;lawsplop&quot; is a play on &quot;slop,&quot; internet slang for low-quality, mass-produced AI content, applied here to legal filings. Courts worldwide have begun issuing sanctions against lawyers who submit such documents, and researchers maintain databases tracking these hallucination cases. This news reflects a broader pattern in which AI-generated volume overwhelms human review capacity in professions built on careful reading.
