## [Tencent open-sources Hy4 flash, a 224B-parameter multimodal model family](https://www.aibase.com/news/32380) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Tencent Hunyuan has open-sourced the Hy4 flash series on GitHub, Hugging Face, ModelScope and GitCode, releasing a standard version, an FP8-quantized version, and a from-scratch Base version, plus GGUF files for local deployment. The model has 224B total parameters \(including 64B Engram parameters\) but only 12B active parameters per token, and Tencent says larger Hy4 models are coming. A major Chinese tech company releasing a full 224B-class model family — including quantized and base checkpoints — under an open license gives developers a high-capability alternative they can self-host rather than only access through an API. The FP8 and GGUF variants in particular lower the hardware barrier for cost-sensitive and on-premise deployments, intensifying competition among open-weight Chinese model families such as Qwen and DeepSeek. Hy4 flash is natively multimodal, accepting text and image input with a context window of up to 1M tokens and decoding speeds above 150 tokens per second according to Tencent&\#x27;s model page. Because only 12B parameters are active per token, it is positioned as the most cost-effective model in the Hy family, and the separate Base checkpoint published &quot;from scratch&quot; gives researchers a clean starting point for further training.

aibase · AIbase · Nov 18, 16:01

**Background**: Hy4 flash belongs to Tencent&\#x27;s Hunyuan \(Hy\) model family, and its &quot;Engram&quot; parameters are a large pool of parameters that are only partially activated per token, a mixture-of-experts-like design that keeps inference cheap despite the large total size. FP8 quantization stores model weights in 8-bit floating point, roughly halving memory use versus 16-bit weights so that big models fit on fewer GPUs, while GGUF is the single-file format introduced by llama.cpp that is now read by nearly every local inference tool including Ollama, LM Studio and llama.cpp itself.

## [curl&\#x27;s Lead Maintainer Asks Whether AI Rust Rewrites Can Keep Up](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Nov/18/daniel-stenberg/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Daniel Stenberg, curl&\#x27;s lead maintainer, reported that while AI agents spent a little over three weeks converting 185,000 lines of C into 340,000 lines of Rust, the real curl project merged over 300 commits in the same window, including more than 200 bugfixes and several vulnerability fixes. He closed by asking whether those AI-generated &quot;clones&quot; can ever keep up with the pace of upstream maintenance. The observation is a credible counterweight to hype around AI-driven &quot;vibe porting&quot; of major open-source C codebases, pointing out that a one-shot translation captures a static snapshot rather than a living, continuously patched project. It matters for anyone weighing whether an AI-generated Rust rewrite actually delivers the security benefits associated with Rust, since those benefits erode if the port never absorbs upstream bug and CVE fixes. The numbers Stenberg cites are 185,000 lines of C becoming 340,000 lines of Rust, alongside 300+ merged commits, 200+ bugfixes and a handful of vulnerability fixes in roughly three weeks, with another ~200 commits expected in the following month. The core caveat is that none of those Rust rewrites contain any of the 200 bugfixes, so their bug count and security posture are frozen at the moment of translation.

rss · Simon Willison · Nov 18, 15:58

**Background**: curl is a ubiquitous open-source library and command-line tool for transferring data across network protocols such as HTTP and FTP, and it is written in C, a language where manual memory management makes buffer overflows and use-after-free bugs common. Rust&\#x27;s ownership and borrowing rules enforce memory safety at compile time, which is why many large C codebases are being partially or fully ported to Rust. Recent AI coding agents have made ambitious rewrites feasible at the push of a button — for example, translations of large Zig or C projects into Rust — but such ports reproduce a codebase as it stood at one point in time rather than tracking its ongoing fixes.
