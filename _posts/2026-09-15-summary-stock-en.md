Texas state regulators approved ExxonMobil&\#x27;s $5 billion carbon capture and storage project, ending a lengthy dispute over safety standards and subsidies. The decision clears the regulatory path for the oil major to proceed with the large-scale CO2 capture project in the state. The approval is a material step for ExxonMobil&\#x27;s low-carbon business and for the wider CCS sector, which oil majors have positioned as a central pillar of their climate strategies even as many announced projects fail to materialise. It also signals that Texas regulators remain willing to greenlight large CO2 infrastructure, reinforcing the state&\#x27;s role as the leading US hub for carbon capture and storage. The FT reports the fight centred on safety concerns and subsidies, reflecting two persistent fault lines for CCS: that projects typically depend on government support such as US tax credits, and that injecting and storing CO2 underground raises long-term leakage and monitoring questions. rss · FT Markets · Sep 15, 19:20

**Background**: Carbon capture and storage \(CCS\) is a process in which CO2 produced by industrial facilities or power plants is separated before it reaches the atmosphere, then transported and injected into deep geological formations for long-term storage. The technology has been used by oil and gas companies since the mid-20th century, initially to purify natural gas and boost oil output, and about 80% of captured CO2 today is used for enhanced oil recovery, which is why the approach is often called CCUS. Governments in the US, Canada, Denmark, China and the UK have programmes to support or mandate it, and almost all operating projects have relied on public financial backing. Environmental groups argue CCS is an unproven and expensive technology that prolongs fossil fuel dependence, while supporters see it as one of the few options for cutting emissions from heavy industry and existing plants.
