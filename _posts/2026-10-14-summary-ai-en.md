## [AI-driven mass decompilation of games sparks preservation boom and IP fears](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Oct/14/ai-is-mass-decompiling-games/) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Indie game developer Popcar published an essay arguing that a recent wave of AI-powered decompilation of existing games is enabling novel mashups between titles and fueling a boom in game preservation projects that get old games running on new hardware, often in the browser via WebAssembly. Simon Willison amplified the piece, noting it captures both the upside and the risk of the trend. Because AI can now plausibly decompile any compiled software, not just old games, the technique threatens to upend software intellectual property norms: paid apps could be open-sourced against their owners&\#x27; will and competitors could lift each other&\#x27;s code, while at the same time giving users a way to keep playing software that publishers abandon. The argument rests on the fact that decompilation reverses compilation to recover near-source-level code, and that AI models — including coding agents and LLMs — make this process far cheaper and more scalable than manual reverse engineering; WebAssembly is highlighted as the practical target that lets decompiled games run portably in browsers.

rss · Simon Willison · Oct 14, 16:26

**Background**: Decompilation is the reverse-engineering of a compiled executable back into a high-level, human-readable form, a practice dating back to the 1960s that is inherently lossy because variable names and other information disappear during compilation. It has traditionally been slow, expert-driven work used for recovering lost source code, migrating programs, and modding. WebAssembly is an open standard portable binary format that lets high-performance code compiled from languages like C, C++ and Rust run efficiently in browsers and other platforms, which is why it is a natural home for resurrected games. AI coding agents and LLMs change the economics by automating the tedious renaming, retyping and structural cleanup that decompilation workflows have always required.

A study published in Nature reports the use of artificial intelligence to perform clinical phenotyping of human brown adipose tissue \(BAT\). The work aims to turn BAT characteristics into measurable, clinically meaningful traits that can support metabolic research and diagnostics. Brown fat is a metabolically active tissue linked to energy expenditure, obesity, and cardiometabolic disease, so a scalable AI-driven way to characterize it in patients could accelerate both metabolic research and the development of BAT-targeted therapies. It also reflects the broader trend of AI moving from image segmentation toward clinically interpretable phenotyping in medical imaging. Clinical phenotyping means deriving defined biochemical or physical traits \(such as a disease or measurable physical characteristic\) from patient data, and BAT is notoriously difficult to quantify because it is small, cold-activated, and heterogeneous across individuals. Existing detection methods rely mainly on PET/CT with radiotracers, while emerging approaches include MRI-based techniques such as chemical exchange saturation transfer \(CEST\) and machine-learning segmentation networks.

google\_news · Nature · Oct 14, 17:59

**Background**: Brown adipose tissue is a specialized fat that burns nutrients to generate heat, and its activity is considered a potential therapeutic target for metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and some cancers. Because BAT is hard to detect and measure consistently in humans, researchers have long sought non-invasive imaging biomarkers for it. Artificial intelligence and machine learning are increasingly applied to medical images to automatically segment and quantify such tissues, forming the basis for &\#x27;phenotyping&\#x27; patients at scale. The Nature paper sits at the intersection of these two lines of work, applying AI to define BAT-related clinical traits.

Nature has published an article titled &quot;Advancing subseasonal forecasting with machine learning,&quot; signaling the journal&\#x27;s attention to machine-learning methods applied to forecasts in the two-week-to-several-month range that lies between conventional weather prediction and seasonal climate outlooks. The item currently surfaces only as a headline link, without an available abstract or accompanying discussion. If machine learning can meaningfully extend useful skill there, the payoff would be broad societal and economic value across sectors that currently rely on unreliable two-to-six-week outlooks. google\_news · Nature · Oct 14, 16:31

**Background**: Subseasonal to seasonal \(S2S\) forecasting refers to predictions spanning roughly two weeks to several months ahead, filling the gap between short-term weather forecasting and long-term climate projections. Unlike day-to-day weather, this window is governed largely by slowly evolving signals such as the Madden-Julian Oscillation, El Niño-Southern Oscillation, soil moisture, snow cover, and stratospheric conditions, which are harder for traditional numerical weather models to capture. Machine learning has recently been applied both as a standalone predictor and as a post-processing correction layer on top of physics-based dynamical models, often at far lower computational cost.
