A sharp sell-off in the US Treasury market is now feeding through to the corporate credit market, driving up borrowing costs for junk-rated US companies. Falling Treasury prices and rising yields are being transmitted into higher yields and coupons on below-investment-grade corporate debt. Higher funding costs squeeze the cash flows of already-leveraged, lower-rated borrowers, making refinancing more expensive and raising the risk of defaults. Because junk-rated firms are the most rate-sensitive part of the credit market, the move is an early signal of broader credit-market stress that could ripple into equity markets and the wider economy. Junk bonds are debt rated below investment grade — typically BB+/Ba1 or lower — and compensate investors for higher default risk with elevated yields. The report gives no specific figures such as basis-point moves or index levels, so the magnitude of the increase in borrowing costs is not yet quantified.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 6, 04:00

**Background**: US Treasuries are considered the closest thing to a risk-free benchmark, so their yields form the base rate off which virtually all other debt — including corporate bonds — is priced. A bond sell-off happens when heavy selling pushes prices down and yields up, often reflecting changing expectations for interest rates, inflation, or government borrowing needs. Junk bonds, also called high-yield bonds, are issued by newer or financially weaker companies; when the risk-free benchmark rises, the yields demanded of these riskier issuers typically rise even more.
