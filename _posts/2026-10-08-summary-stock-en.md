## [UK property fund investors face three-month wait under new FCA rules](https://www.ft.com/content/96474d3e-7d61-4327-b6c2-ed37eecad3f8?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 7.0/10

The Financial Conduct Authority \(FCA\) is introducing new rules that will require UK investors in open-ended property funds to wait up to three months before they can recoup their money, rather than redeeming immediately. The change is designed to avoid a repeat of the trading suspensions that hit the sector during past bouts of market turmoil. Open-ended property funds have long been a mainstream holding for UK retail investors and advisers, so shifting them from daily dealing to a notice period materially changes how savers access their money. The reform should reduce the risk of damaging fund suspensions and forced asset sales, but it also makes these funds less liquid for investors and could push flows toward exchange-traded REITs instead. The FCA has consulted on notice periods of between 90 and 180 days, so the final three-month requirement sits at the shorter end of that range; regulators have also warned that notice periods will not prevent every suspension. The FCA has previously argued that open-ended structures holding directly-owned property suffer from a structural &quot;liquidity mismatch&quot; because buildings cannot be sold quickly enough to meet daily redemptions.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 8, 14:03

**Background**: Open-ended property funds let investors buy and sell units on a daily basis, yet the underlying assets are physical buildings that can take months or years to sell — a classic example of an illiquid asset. When markets seized up, for example around the 2016 Brexit vote and the March 2020 Covid crash, several UK funds had to suspend redemptions because they could not sell properties fast enough to pay departing investors. The Bank of England&\#x27;s Financial Stability Report flagged this mismatch as a potential systemic risk, noting that around 95 per cent of UK open-ended funds offered daily redemptions, which prompted the FCA to push through structural reform.

An FT analysis argues that banks&\#x27; trading businesses are now booming not because the banks are placing big bets themselves, but because they are lending to hedge funds in what has become a trillion-dollar borrowing spree. Post-crisis regulation has pushed banks out of proprietary trading and into financing their clients&\#x27; leveraged positions instead. This shift turns prime brokerage and hedge fund financing into a core profit engine for large banks, meaning Wall Street&\#x27;s earnings increasingly depend on the leverage taken on by a relatively small number of funds. It also concentrates risk outside the regulated banking balance sheet, so a sharp unwind in hedge fund positions could hit bank trading revenues and, in a severe scenario, financial stability. **Background**: Prime brokerage is the bundled service package that large banks offer hedge funds, including financing, securities lending, centralised clearing and custody, which lets funds run leveraged strategies without holding assets directly. After the 2008 crisis, the Dodd-Frank Act&\#x27;s Volcker Rule barred banks that take FDIC-insured deposits from speculative proprietary trading, so banks moved from betting their own capital to financing other people&\#x27;s bets. That is why trading desks can post strong revenue while the actual market risk sits with the hedge funds.

It also repeats that the market should play a decisive role in exchange rate formation while the authorities retain a managed floating framework, an internal tension worth noting for technically minded readers.
