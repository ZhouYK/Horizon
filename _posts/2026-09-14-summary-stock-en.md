## [Oil Hits $109 as Saudi Arabia Shuts Key Export Pipeline](https://www.ft.com/content/4845a503-3ddd-4bb7-a469-c51f275bc873?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

Oil prices jumped to $109 a barrel after Saudi Arabia shut down a vital export pipeline and withdrew from regional talks aimed at de-escalating hostilities over the Strait of Hormuz, following a drone attack last week. The combination of a physical supply cut and a collapsed diplomatic channel marks a sharp escalation in Middle East tensions. This is a textbook negative supply shock: the Strait of Hormuz normally carries roughly 25% of seaborne oil trade and about 20% of global liquefied natural gas, so any sustained disruption threatens worldwide energy prices, shipping costs and inflation. Because oil is a key input for nearly every industry, sustained prices near $109 could feed into transport, food and manufacturing costs well beyond the Gulf region. The pipeline that was closed normally serves as an alternative export route that lets Gulf producers move crude without transiting the Strait of Hormuz, so shutting it removes a key bypass and concentrates risk in the chokepoint itself. Saudi Arabia&\#x27;s withdrawal from the de-escalation talks also eliminates the main diplomatic off-ramp that had been containing the crisis, leaving few mechanisms to lower tensions.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 14, 20:11

**Background**: The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and the Musandam Peninsula of Oman and the UAE, and it is the only sea passage from the Persian Gulf to the open ocean. It is one of the world&\#x27;s most important energy chokepoints, and it is also the only maritime route for several Gulf countries including the UAE, Qatar, Bahrain, Kuwait and Iraq. A supply shock is an event that suddenly changes the supply of a commodity, shifting prices sharply; the 1973 OPEC oil embargo is the classic example of how an oil supply shock can trigger stagflation, combining rising prices with falling output.

## [US 10-Year Treasury Yield Tops 5% for First Time Since 2023](https://www.ft.com/content/06cb850d-089a-4318-88d7-a410e9766b89?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

The US 10-year Treasury yield breached the 5% mark for the first time since 2023, a level the Financial Times describes as moving toward territory not seen in decades, as an inflation shock stemming from the Iran war continues to linger. This makes the world&\#x27;s most widely watched financial benchmark decisively more expensive for governments, companies and households to borrow against. The 10-year Treasury yield is the global benchmark risk-free rate, so a move to 5% compresses equity valuations — especially for high-multiple growth stocks — while raising corporate and mortgage borrowing costs and typically strengthening the dollar. Tying the move to a persistent supply-side inflation shock from the Iran war implies central banks may stay hawkish for longer, amplifying the impact across asset classes rather than confining it to one sector. The 5% threshold was last crossed in 2023, and the current move is framed as heading toward levels not seen in decades, underscoring how unusual this rate environment is relative to the post-2008 era. The key caveat is that this is a single headline yield level — the durability of the move depends on whether the inflation shock proves transitory or becomes embedded in expectations.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 14, 20:08

**Background**: A Treasury yield is the return investors demand to lend money to the US government, and because US debt is considered the closest thing to a risk-free asset, its yield anchors pricing for virtually every other asset in global finance. Longer-dated yields like the 10-year are driven mainly by expectations for future inflation and central bank policy rather than by the current overnight rate, so they rise when investors demand compensation for inflation risk. A yield of 5% is notable because the 2010s saw extraordinarily low rates, and many portfolios, valuations and debt structures were built on the assumption that money would stay cheap. Inflation shocks such as a war-driven spike in energy and commodity prices tend to push long-term yields up by making investors demand more compensation to hold bonds.

China&\#x27;s State-owned Assets Supervision and Administration Commission \(SASAC\) issued a notice requiring central state-owned enterprises to take the lead in paying their bills on time, making cash payment a &quot;hard requirement&quot; and banning the issuance of new bills or notes with maturities longer than six months. The directive came alongside August credit data showing new renminbi loans of only 60 billion yuan — down 530 billion yuan year-on-year — and total social financing of 1.66 trillion yuan, below market expectations. Central SOEs sit at the top of many industrial supply chains, so their payment behavior directly determines the cash flow of thousands of small and mid-sized suppliers, and a shift toward cash settlement could ease the chronic problem of overdue receivables. At the same time, the exceptionally weak loan and social financing figures sharpen expectations that Beijing will need to loosen policy further to support credit expansion. August&\#x27;s 60 billion yuan in new renminbi loans was an unusually small figure, down 530 billion yuan from a year earlier, and the 1.66 trillion yuan of total social financing also missed forecasts as weak lending dragged on the aggregate. The SASAC rules specifically target the practice of using long-dated acceptance bills to delay payment, which effectively forces suppliers to bear financing costs.

rss · 财新网 · Sep 14, 22:55

**Background**: Total social financing \(TSF, also called Aggregate Financing to the Real Economy, AFRE\) measures the total funds the real economy obtains from the financial system, including renminbi loans, foreign-currency loans, bond and equity financing, and it is a key gauge of credit expansion. Bank acceptance bills and commercial notes are widely used in China to defer payment between companies, and longer maturities mean suppliers wait longer for cash. SASAC is the State Council body that supervises China&\#x27;s central state-owned enterprises and sets operational and compliance requirements for them.
