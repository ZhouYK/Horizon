## [PBOC Shifts Policy Rate Anchor to 1.25% Overnight Reverse Repo](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-10-30/102499195.html) ⭐️ 9.0/10

On October 30, 2026, the People&\#x27;s Bank of China announced that starting November 2 it will conduct overnight reverse repo auctions every business day using a fixed-rate, quantity-based tender at a current rate of 1.25%, formally making the overnight rate its main policy anchor. At the same time, the 7-day reverse repo was converted into a fixed-quantity, rate-auction tool with multiple-price bidding, to be used mainly around tax periods and month-end. This is a framework-level change that moves China&\#x27;s monetary policy further from quantity-based toward price-based management, and it alters how short-term liquidity and the entire money-market rate curve are anchored. Because other tools such as temporary reverse repos and the repo facility for foreign central bank-type institutions are now priced as spreads over the overnight rate, the change propagates through short-term funding, bond pricing, and broader Chinese asset markets. The spread adjustments applied to other tools are unchanged, only their reference rate is switched from the 7-day to the overnight rate; the 7-day operation becomes a multiple-price auction, so it can produce several winning rates rather than a single policy-signal rate. Market participants quoted by Caixin noted that while the stated goal is to optimize short-end rate control, an actual downward shift in the rate center is an objective result, since the overnight rate strips out term risk premium and delivers a faster, purer policy signal.

rss · 财新网 · Oct 30, 14:06

**Background**: China&\#x27;s central bank conducts open market operations \(OMO\) by lending to primary dealers through reverse repos, and since 2019 the 7-day reverse repo rate had served as the de facto policy rate, with the market-traded DR007 and similar benchmarks hovering around it. In a quantity-based framework the central bank mainly targets how much liquidity to inject; in a price-based framework it instead sets the price of liquidity and lets demand adjust, which is how the Fed, ECB and other major central banks operate. As part of this transition, the PBOC had already been experimenting with overnight reverse repos in 2026, and the 7-day rate&\#x27;s role had been progressively narrowed to a reference for term operations.

## [ExxonMobil and Chevron Post Bumper Profits, Warn on Diesel Supply](https://www.ft.com/content/e934d2dd-9d6c-4099-95a5-1c300ed28cbb?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 8.0/10

ExxonMobil and Chevron reported strong profits driven by soaring refining margins, with the Financial Times attributing much of the margin surge to geopolitical disruption surrounding the Iran conflict. Alongside the earnings, both companies warned that diesel supplies remain constrained and could stay tight. Refining margins are the swing factor in Big Oil earnings, so a margin-driven profit beat signals that the money is being made downstream in fuels rather than upstream in crude. Persistent diesel tightness matters beyond oil investors because diesel powers trucking, farming, construction and mining, so sustained high prices feed directly into freight and food costs. The constraint being flagged is refining capacity and fuel inventories rather than a lack of crude, since diesel tightness has persisted even with record US oil production. Diesel economics are typically tracked via the diesel crack spread — the ULSD futures price times 42 gallons minus the crude price — and the broader 3-2-1 crack spread, which assumes two barrels of gasoline and one of diesel per three barrels of crude.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 30, 10:37

**Background**: Integrated oil majors like ExxonMobil and Chevron operate across the whole value chain: they pump crude \(upstream\), refine it into products such as gasoline, jet fuel and diesel \(downstream\), and sell chemicals. The key downstream metric is the gross refining margin, or crack spread — the gap between what a barrel of crude costs and what the refined products made from it sell for. When product prices rise faster than crude prices, as happens during supply disruptions or refinery outages, that gap widens and refiners earn outsized profits even if crude prices are flat. Diesel is especially prone to squeezes because it is the backbone fuel of freight and heavy industry, global refinery capacity has grown slowly, and inventories can be depleted quickly by seasonal demand or unplanned outages.
