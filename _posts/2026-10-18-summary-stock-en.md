## [China Proposes Stricter Auto Safety Rules With 30,000 km Reliability Tests](https://weekly.caixin.com/2026-10-17/102494225.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

China’s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology and three other departments released a draft notice on October 10, 2026, adding 51 major vehicle technical parameters and an innovation-design technical guide, and barring noncompliant new models from sale. The draft requires reliability validation of at least 30,000 km and environmental-adaptability testing of no less than one year, including a high-cold–high-heat–high-cold sequence. The proposal directly raises compliance and validation costs and lengthens product-development cycles, particularly for NEV startups accustomed to rapid model launches. It signals a regulatory shift from encouraging fast innovation toward forcing automakers to prove durability and safety before sale, which could consolidate the industry and reduce the risk of owners effectively becoming beta testers. The draft’s 51 parameters and innovation-design guide apply to newly declared products involving innovative designs from January 1, 2027, with public comments open until November 10, 2026. Reliability thresholds are not uniform: conventional vehicles face the 30,000 km requirement, while NEVs must follow applicable type-approval test rules and submit type-approval reports—some reports cite a 15,000 km threshold for pure EVs.

**Background**: China is the world’s largest auto market and the leading producer of NEVs, where intense competition has compressed model-development cycles to as little as a year or two. Before a new model can be sold in China, it must pass type-approval tests, including reliability road tests and “three-high” environmental tests in high-temperature, high-cold and high-altitude conditions. The draft responds to concerns that faster iteration and novel designs may outpace validation, leaving safety and durability risks for buyers.

## [China&\#x27;s Power Retailers Face 5 Billion Yuan Loss as Price Bets Backfire](https://weekly.caixin.com/2026-10-18/102494546.html) ⭐️ 7.0/10

In October 2026, nine power-selling companies backed by central state-owned enterprises in Guangxi jointly appealed for relief after low-priced retail contracts turned deeply loss-making when spot electricity prices rose, with the industry projected to lose 5 billion yuan \(50亿元\) for the year. The case exposes a core risk in China&\#x27;s power-market reform: retail sellers that profit from price spreads can be wiped out when medium- and long-term contracts diverge from spot prices, with losses ultimately threatening generators, users, and state-owned utilities. It could also force policy intervention on risk-sharing, contract renegotiation, or price-linkage rules. The contracts were signed at roughly 0.26 yuan/kWh to customers while long-term purchases from generators cost about 0.337 yuan/kWh, and the retailers had bet that spot prices would fall; instead, higher coal and gas prices pushed spot power prices up. The 5 billion yuan \(50亿元\) figure is an industry-wide estimate, and reports say part of the price difference from renewable mechanism electricity has been passed to retailers, adding to their burden.

**Background**: China began allowing retail electricity sellers after the 2015 power-sector reforms, letting companies buy power wholesale through medium- and long-term contracts and resell it to industrial and commercial users. ## [China launches new four-year hydrogen pilot across five city clusters](https://www.caixin.com/2026-10-18/102494558.html) ⭐️ 7.0/10

On October 16, 2026, China&\#x27;s Ministry of Industry and Information Technology \(MIIT\), Ministry of Finance and National Development and Reform Commission jointly issued the Administrative Measures for the Hydrogen Energy Comprehensive Application Pilot, together with a notice approving pilot work plans for five city clusters: Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, the Greater Bay Area, Northeast China \(including eastern Inner Mongolia\) with the Yangtze River Delta, Xinjiang with the Chengdu-Chongqing economic circle, and the Yellow River &\#x27;Jizi Bend&\#x27; with the Central Plains. The four-year pilot shifts beyond transport to four task lists — hydrogen technology and equipment breakthroughs, industrial hydrogen projects, fuel cell vehicle deployment and hydrogen refueling station construction — backed by central funds disbursed through a &\#x27;pre-allocate first, settle later&\#x27; mechanism. This marks a strategic pivot for China&\#x27;s hydrogen policy: the previous round was essentially a fuel cell vehicle demonstration, whereas this round puts industrial hydrogen use and equipment manufacturing at the center, which could unlock far larger volumes of green hydrogen demand and cost reduction. For investors, the concrete, multi-year funding framework gives visibility to hydrogen equipment, fuel cell and refueling infrastructure names across five designated regions. Under the pre-allocation model, the central government disburses a portion of the award money once a city cluster&\#x27;s plan is approved, easing the working-capital and financing pressure on the hydrogen supply chain, with the remainder settled after performance review. Clusters that underperform can face mandated plan adjustments, deducted or suspended reward funds, or even loss of pilot status, and they must upload project data to a national pilot management platform; local reports put the award ceiling at 1.6 billion yuan per city cluster.

rss · 财新网 · Oct 18, 22:58

**Background**: China&\#x27;s earlier hydrogen push centered on a fuel cell vehicle demonstration program launched in 2021 across city clusters such as Beijing-Tianjin-Hebei, Shanghai, Guangdong, Hebei and Henan, using a reward-in-place-of-subsidy model. That round got the industry from zero to one but exposed a bottleneck: transport uses relatively little hydrogen, so it could not drive large-scale cost reduction of green hydrogen, while high hydrogen supply costs in turn constrained vehicle rollout. The new pilot responds by adding industrial hydrogen projects and equipment breakthroughs to the vehicle and refueling-station lists, aiming to build a complete industrial chain while curbing local protectionism.

According to a Financial Times report, demand for physical cash is rising again in India, driven by growth in the country&\#x27;s shadow economy, while a proposed new charge on transactions threatens the popularity of the UPI digital payments system. The report signals a potential reversal in India&\#x27;s long-standing push toward a less-cash economy. UPI is the backbone of India&\#x27;s digital payments boom and is often cited globally as a model for state-built instant payment rails, so any fee that dents its adoption could slow financial inclusion and change how billions of transactions flow. Rising cash demand also complicates the government&\#x27;s tax-compliance and formalisation agenda, with implications for fintech firms, banks and merchants that have built businesses on UPI rails. rss · FT Markets · Oct 18, 04:11

**Background**: UPI \(Unified Payments Interface\) is an instant payment system and protocol built by the National Payments Corporation of India in 2016; it links multiple bank accounts into a single mobile app and has become one of the world&\#x27;s highest-volume real-time payment networks. India&\#x27;s 2016 demonetisation experiment, which withdrew high-value banknotes overnight to curb black money, made the country a closely watched test case for whether digital payments can displace cash. The &\#x27;shadow economy&\#x27; refers to economic activity that goes unreported and outside formal taxation and regulation, and it typically relies on cash to avoid scrutiny.
