## [Anthropic&\#x27;s Claude now refuses to clone or reverse-engineer existing games](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiyAJBVV95cUxQQVBYaTFvbkpGT0FNbXhiUmMwdHR3SlRQYVdwbWFRalZ0SEtSd0pSQ2FOOFhsazRtY1VCX19heHpvTzEwSkJGNFQ1b1lBOXJmU1M2NGdvRnZyMEVNaW9YbnpBS1pSMC1ET3JnR0s1WFE0WmluRldpemhYUUM0elI2YzFfQWFQWDZ5UlFWQUplbVBId1JDNGtCQUoyMHROcVlSbXZHMUxremtlaFd2cy1qRmc0eVJfYmM0Rm95SHdScVVUS2NBMW1vMFRsNUZsUk5ILXZ4dWdsVVFIRjZhU0ZxaGNyRkFtZnFUS2FpbUpDQ25ydWtoZkF6SHdiT252SDhTOWdGeERvNTU5SkxWRlFGOWluZGM3SEVGVW14ZHNtWTVmR2VkUlh1UFFZcjM3akFuRENWT3VhQTJ5clNnQ1JyNVpibU1oNlRm?oc=5) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Anthropic confirmed it has updated the prompts behind Claude Code&\#x27;s Opus 5.5 model so the assistant steers away from reproducing or cloning specific existing video games, citing intellectual-property infringement as a violation of its usage policy. Tom&\#x27;s Hardware reports that the changes go well beyond game cloning, with developers saying the new restrictions also block reverse-engineering work and even routine troubleshooting tasks. The change shows a major AI provider tightening behavioral guardrails at the model-prompt level rather than through a formal policy document, which can silently break workflows developers already depend on. Because Claude Code is widely used for agentic coding on real codebases, overly broad refusals around reverse engineering and debugging could push teams toward competing tools or locally hosted open-weight models. Anthropic says original games, genre-based games, and projects built from a user&\#x27;s own designs and assets should still work normally, and that it will keep tuning the rules. The restrictions are implemented as prompt-level guidance in Claude Code rather than as a hard filter, so behaviour may vary by context and is likely to shift as Anthropic iterates.

google\_news · Tom&\#x27;s Hardware · Oct 31, 12:20

**Background**: Claude Code is Anthropic&\#x27;s agentic coding tool, which developers run from the terminal or an IDE to let Claude read a codebase, edit files and execute commands. Opus 5.5 is Anthropic&\#x27;s current flagship model for demanding reasoning, coding and long-horizon agentic work, so its behaviour directly shapes what the tool will and won&\#x27;t do. Reverse engineering — decompiling or analysing binaries to understand how software works — is a long-standing practice in security research, malware analysis and interoperability work, and is often restricted by software licences and NDAs, which is why AI vendors now have to draw lines around it.

Texas A&amp;M University plans to open a fully automated metallurgy laboratory in which AI software and robotic workers carry out alloy experiments largely without human hands, according to Houston Public Media. The lab is presented as a way to speed up the design, synthesis and testing of new metal alloys rather than relying on slow manual bench work. Alloy development traditionally takes years or decades of trial-and-error testing, so an autonomous, round-the-clock lab could compress discovery cycles for materials used in aerospace, energy and defense applications. It also signals that the &quot;self-driving lab&quot; model, already spreading in chemistry and materials science, is moving into a more traditional heavy-industry discipline like metallurgy. The report offers few technical specifics — no opening date, budget, or list of target alloy systems is given — so the concrete research output of the facility remains unknown. The general concept mirrors existing self-driving labs that pair machine-learning experiment planning with robotic sample handling and automated analytical instruments in a closed loop.

google\_news · Houston Public Media · Oct 31, 13:00

**Background**: Metallurgy has long been an empirical field: researchers melt, cast, heat-treat and mechanically test alloys one batch at a time, which makes searching a vast compositional space extremely slow. Self-driving laboratories \(SDLs\) aim to change that by combining machine learning with lab automation and robotics, so that models propose the next experiment, robots run it, and the resulting data feeds back into the model — a closed loop described in recent reviews of SDL technology in chemistry and materials science. Applying this to alloys is harder than for liquid-phase chemistry because of high temperatures, long processing steps and complex multi-scale microstructure-property relationships.

## [New York Enacts AI Safety Law Restricting Chatbot Features for Minors](https://news.google.com/rss/articles/CBMiwAFBVV95cUxPbTF0SVdxTlFmUGhlQ2pXMFlYeTJsb2t2UUMwTlBuNkw4aUQxXzZ4bHRJRmpfYWszTHlOVUhObTlkdGFfTTNBckxwSzdLeGpDUXVYallrN0pOLVZReGNrNVdpQ3FBZGl6Q2t2NGVPTEs2bXAtZ09ZU0o5SzZmZGExRjgyQ3A2WXdjaVBtMk5hc3pfZVhOTXhDd1pZalpkdzlnZkJmMm9TY3YxdjZHWHdQOVhtMHY2TG4wcE9UR1lYRDg?oc=5) ⭐️ 6.0/10

New York State Senator Kristen Gonzalez&\#x27;s bill banning an unsafe chatbot feature for minors has been signed into law, according to an announcement published by the New York State Senate. The Senate framed the legislation as a leading AI safety bill, with Gonzalez credited as its sponsor. This makes New York one of the first states to impose a legally binding restriction on how AI chatbot products may behave toward minors, creating concrete compliance obligations for companies that ship conversational AI to consumers. Because US federal AI legislation remains stalled, state-level rules like this one tend to become de facto national standards once large platforms decide it is easier to apply one policy everywhere than to maintain separate state builds. The Senate&\#x27;s announcement is headline-level only: it does not disclose the bill number, the effective date, the enforcement mechanism or penalties, or the technical definition of the prohibited &quot;unsafe chatbot feature,&quot; all of which will determine how heavily it bites. As with other state laws regulating AI-generated speech, its practical scope is likely to depend on how narrowly the banned feature is defined and on how courts weigh First Amendment challenges.

google\_news · The New York State Senate \(.gov\) · Oct 30, 23:19

**Background**: AI &quot;companion&quot; or character chatbots — products that let users hold open-ended, emotionally inflected conversations with simulated personalities — have become enormously popular with teenagers. That popularity has drawn mounting scrutiny: a wave of lawsuits and press reports have linked intense teen engagement with these apps to self-harm and other harms, and US regulators have opened inquiries into how companion chatbots are marketed and safeguarded for young users. In response, some vendors have already restricted open-ended chat for under-18 accounts, while legislators at the state level have moved faster than Congress in writing rules for the category.
