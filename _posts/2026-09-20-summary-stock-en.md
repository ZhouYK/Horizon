## [Iran War Fuels Global Motor Oil Shortage; US Retailers Ration Supplies](https://www.ft.com/content/64f90ab8-5fb0-4594-a900-d03e5ca051e3?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The Iran war has caused a global shortage of the lubricant used in vehicle engines, driving motor oil prices to roughly quadruple and forcing US retailers to ration how much customers can buy as supplies run dry. This is a classic commodity supply shock that feeds directly into inflation: motor oil is a non-discretionary input for car owners, repair shops, trucking fleets and industrial operators, so quadrupling prices and rationing raise operating costs across transport and logistics. It also shows how a geopolitical conflict can disrupt seemingly secondary refined products, not just crude oil and fuels, transmitting war risk into everyday consumer budgets. Lubricants are a comparatively small-volume but high-value refined product whose supply depends on a limited number of suitable base-oil refineries and additive supply chains, making shortfalls hard to substitute quickly. The FT report focuses on the price surge and retail rationing, but offers limited detail on the volumes affected, the specific rationing limits imposed, or how long the disruption is expected to last.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 20, 10:00

**Background**: Motor oil is an engine lubricant made mainly from base oils refined from crude petroleum, blended with chemical additives that control viscosity, wear and oxidation. Because lubricant-grade base oils come from a relatively small set of refineries and trade heavily through the Gulf region, a conflict involving Iran can interrupt both production and shipping routes such as the Strait of Hormuz. Unlike gasoline or diesel, lubricants are harder to swap between suppliers because engines and equipment are formulated for specific specifications, so shortages can persist even after crude prices stabilize. The result for consumers is higher prices at auto-parts stores and service shops, and for businesses higher maintenance and fleet costs.

The SEC has proposed rolling back a key post-Enron audit requirement by raising the exemption threshold for Section 404\(b\) internal-control attestation from $700mn to $2bn in market capitalisation, a change that would exempt a large swath of mid-cap US-listed companies from independent auditor review of their internal controls. Accounting firms are lobbying against the proposal, which they say would put roughly $400mn in annual US audit fees at risk. The change would shift a meaningful compliance cost off mid-sized public companies but simultaneously remove a revenue stream that has been central to the audit business model since 2002, potentially intensifying price competition among accounting firms. It also raises a broader policy question about how much independent verification of internal controls investors should be entitled to, at a time when the Trump administration is broadly pursuing deregulation across financial oversight. rss · FT Markets · Sep 20, 10:00

**Background**: Section 404 of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act was introduced in 2002 after the Enron accounting fraud and the collapse of auditor Arthur Andersen, and it obliges public companies to document and test their internal financial controls. Section 404\(b\) goes further by requiring an independent external auditor to attest to those controls, a task that generates substantial recurring fees for accounting firms. SOX also created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board \(PCAOB\), a nonprofit body that oversees audits of US-listed companies and is itself now a target of deregulatory pressure.

The analysis emphasizes a widening divergence: crude is expensive, diesel is more expensive, and some by-products dependent on crude refining are tighter still, while the $200-per-barrel prediction that circulated in the first three months of the war failed to materialize because supply routes and inventories adapted outside the conflict zone. **Background**: The Strait of Hormuz is the narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which roughly a fifth of the world&\#x27;s seaborne oil passes, making it the single most important chokepoint in global energy trade. Brent crude is the international benchmark price for seaborne oil, and diesel is a middle distillate refined from crude that powers trucks, ships, farming equipment and backup generators. A sustained inability to restore normal Hormuz traffic therefore raises both the cost of crude and the cost of turning it into the fuels that keep goods moving.
