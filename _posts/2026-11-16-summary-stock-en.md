## [S&amp;P warns of forced deleveraging risk as European banks lean on SRTs](https://www.ft.com/content/3b6cccb1-cac2-4e64-a2c2-3b3801f9a5fb?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 7.0/10

S&amp;P Global Ratings has warned that European banks are becoming increasingly reliant on significant risk transfers \(SRTs\) to boost returns and free up lending capacity, which raises the sector&\#x27;s collective exposure to forced deleveraging. The report frames this dependence as a sector-wide vulnerability rather than an issue confined to individual institutions. SRTs have become a core capital-management tool for European banks, so a broad pullback by the private credit funds and other investors who take on this risk could force many lenders to shrink balance sheets and cut lending at the same time. That makes the SRT market a systemic channel worth monitoring for bank investors, credit analysts and anyone tracking euro-area credit supply. SRT structures leave the underlying loans on the bank&\#x27;s balance sheet while transferring a tranche of their credit risk to investors through credit derivatives or financial guarantees, so the capital relief depends on that investor demand persisting. The warning carries no specific figures or named institutions in the available summary, but it echoes earlier flags from the BIS and the ESRB about the rapid growth of synthetic risk transfers and the opacity of the concentrated investor base.

rss · FT Markets · Nov 16, 05:00

**Background**: A significant risk transfer is a type of securitisation in which a bank pays investors — typically private credit funds, specialist credit managers and insurers — to take on part of the credit risk in a loan portfolio, usually a mezzanine tranche. Because regulators accept that the risk has genuinely moved off the bank, the bank can reduce the capital it must hold against those loans, freeing capacity for new lending and improving reported returns. The catch is that this capital relief is not permanent: it depends on the appetite of a relatively small pool of repeat investors. Forced deleveraging describes the situation in which funding, collateral or risk constraints leave an institution no room to keep its existing exposure, so it must sell assets or cut lending — the mechanism that turns a market shock into a credit contraction.
