## [European gas tops €100 as Iran war fears meet thin winter storage](https://www.ft.com/content/afe6e244-8433-4371-a26f-7aacbc9b8dcd?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 8.0/10

The move was compounded by forecasts of a cold winter arriving while European gas storage sits at unusually low levels for the time of year. The €100 level is a psychologically and economically significant threshold: sustained prices at this level feed directly into European electricity bills, industrial costs and headline inflation, and would squeeze utilities and energy-intensive manufacturers already weakened by the loss of cheap Russian pipeline gas. Because Europe now competes with Asia for LNG cargoes, a Middle East supply disruption would transmit price shocks well beyond the continent&\#x27;s borders. The headline figure refers to the TTF front-month contract, the Dutch virtual trading hub that serves as the pricing benchmark for both pipeline gas and regasified LNG across continental Europe. The rally rests on two reinforcing factors — geopolitical risk premium from a potential wider Iran war and a physically tight storage buffer — meaning prices could stay volatile even if tensions de-escalate, since inventories cannot be rebuilt quickly before peak winter demand.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 28, 17:00

**Background**: Europe&\#x27;s gas market is priced mainly off the TTF \(Title Transfer Facility\), a Dutch virtual trading point where traders exchange futures and physical volumes; it became the continent&\#x27;s dominant benchmark after the 2022 energy crisis shifted Europe away from Russian pipeline supply toward seaborne LNG. Gas storage levels act as the system&\#x27;s shock absorber — when they enter winter well below normal, the market has less ability to cope with a supply disruption or a cold snap, and prices must rise to attract extra LNG cargoes away from Asian buyers. That is why a geopolitical event in the Middle East, a major LNG exporting region, can move European prices so sharply even though little Iranian gas reaches Europe directly.

## [ETFs use perpetual futures to bet on private AI companies like Anthropic and OpenAI](https://www.ft.com/content/b5407b4e-3f63-451b-95b9-d7162ed3d0bf?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Newly authorised perpetual-style derivatives, known as &quot;perps&quot;, are now being used inside ETFs to give investors exposure to private companies such as Anthropic and OpenAI without the fund owning the underlying shares. The recent wave of launches marks the latest attempt to package highly illiquid private stocks into exchange-traded products. Private AI leaders like OpenAI and Anthropic are among the most sought-after assets in the market, but retail and most institutional investors have had almost no way to access them; ETFs built on perps could democratise that exposure. At the same time, it pushes derivatives-based synthetic exposure into mainstream fund wrappers, raising questions for regulators and ETF providers about how such products are priced and disclosed. Perpetual futures have no expiry date and rely on periodic funding payments to track an underlying price, and here they let the ETF deliver synthetic exposure without owning illiquid private shares. Because there is no liquid public market for the underlying private stock, the products depend on reference prices and counterparty arrangements, so tracking error, valuation opacity and counterparty risk are notable caveats.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 28, 05:00

**Background**: Perpetual futures, or &quot;perps&quot;, are derivative contracts that track an asset&\#x27;s price without ever expiring; unlike traditional futures, which settle on a set date, perps use periodic funding payments between longs and shorts to keep their price near spot. They originated and became dominant in crypto trading and are now migrating into regulated financial products. Private companies like OpenAI and Anthropic have no publicly traded shares, so gaining exposure to them normally requires buying into secondary-market transactions or waiting for an IPO. An ETF is a fund that trades on an exchange, and using perps inside one is a way to offer exposure to assets the fund cannot directly own.

## [A-Share M&amp;A Bet Failures: Xinghui Wins 102 Million Yuan First-Instance Verdict](https://weekly.caixin.com/2026-10-24/102496750.html) ⭐️ 6.0/10

On October 16, 2026, ChiNext-listed Xinghui Co. \(300464.SZ\) disclosed that the Foshan Intermediate People&\#x27;s Court in Guangdong issued a first-instance ruling ordering Sun Caijin and other former shareholders of Shenzhen Zelbao Technology to pay performance compensation and penalty interest totaling roughly 102 million yuan, arising from the failure to meet earn-out targets on a 1.53 billion yuan cross-sector acquisition completed in 2018. The company has won at first instance, but actual collection of the money is still uncertain and may take considerable time. The case is a prominent example of a systemic problem: since 2020 nearly 700 listed companies have disclosed more than 840 major asset restructurings, over 410 of which carried performance-commitment \(earn-out\) agreements, so failed bets now pose broad legal and investment risk across the M&amp;A sector and individual stocks with outstanding commitments. It also highlights the unresolved legal question of whether a failed commitment is merely a civil breach of contract or can escalate into criminal proceedings, which affects how investors should price such risk. Xinghui acquired Shenzhen Zelbao for 1.53 billion yuan in cash plus shares with a three-year earn-out; the dispute erupted in March 2021 when Xinghui disclosed historical tax risks at Zelbao&\#x27;s overseas subsidiary, citing tax evasion as the reason the targets were missed, while Sun Caijin&\#x27;s side countersued for infringement and demanded the release of locked-up exchange shares. The 102 million yuan figure is only a provisional first-instance total, and as is common in A-share compensation disputes, the winning company may still struggle to collect if the obligors&\#x27; shares are frozen or pledged and their assets are restricted.

**Background**: A performance-commitment or earn-out agreement — known in Chinese practice as a &quot;duidu&quot; \(bet-on\) agreement, formally a Valuation Adjustment Mechanism \(VAM\) — is a contract in which the seller of a target company promises future profit levels and agrees to compensate the buyer in shares or cash if those targets are missed. Such clauses became standard in A-share M&amp;A because they let buyers pay high valuations for targets while hedging uncertainty, but when the promised profits fail to materialize, the parties frequently end up in litigation over compensation amounts, share unlocks, and the boundary between civil liability and criminal fraud. Chinese courts generally uphold commitments made between shareholders, while compensation is typically settled first with shares and then with cash.
