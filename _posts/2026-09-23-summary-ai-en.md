## [McKinsey Warns AI Agents May Cost 30x More Than Chat AI](https://www.aibase.com/news/31311) ⭐️ 7.0/10

McKinsey has published a warning that enterprise deployments of AI agents could cost as much as 30 times more than conventional chat-based AI, because agents must actually execute tasks rather than just generate text. The firm notes that agentic tasks are typically decomposed into multiple steps, and that different execution paths toward the same goal can vary enormously in cost, creating a new and hard-to-forecast budget pressure for companies. The warning lands as enterprises rush to move from pilot chatbots to autonomous agents, and it suggests that AI enthusiasm inside companies could produce runaway operating expenses if cost governance is not built in from the start. It affects engineering leaders, platform teams and finance departments that are currently approving agent roadmaps on the assumption that costs will scale much like chat AI. The core driver is multi-step execution: a single agent request may trigger many chained model calls plus tool invocations, retries and verification steps, each of which consumes inference tokens. Because the same objective can be reached through wildly different paths — some short, some meandering — the per-task cost is far harder to predict or cap than the per-message cost of a chatbot.

aibase · AIbase · Sep 23, 18:01

**Background**: AI agents are systems that autonomously pursue a goal by planning workflows and using available tools, in contrast to chatbots that respond to a single prompt within a preset script. This planning-and-execution loop is what makes agents useful for complex enterprise workflows, but it also means the model is invoked repeatedly rather than once, and each invocation bills tokens to the customer. McKinsey&\#x27;s point is that this architectural difference translates directly into an economic one, so ROI comparisons built on chat AI usage patterns will understate the real bill.

## [Fei-Fei Li: AI Safety Should Not Be Judged Only by Developers](https://www.aibase.com/news/31296) ⭐️ 7.0/10

Fei-Fei Li is publicly arguing that safety assessments of powerful AI systems should not be left solely to the developers who build them, and she is calling on independent institutions, government, industry, and academia to play a stronger oversight role. She acknowledges that internal research can evaluate individual models, but contends that this cannot substitute for broader, cross-sector safety evaluation. The proposal goes to the core of the AI governance debate: whether self-regulation by labs is sufficient or whether external, independent auditing is needed as models grow more capable. If adopted, such a shift would affect frontier AI developers, regulators, and any organization deploying high-risk models, and it aligns with a broader trend toward third-party evaluation and mandatory reporting requirements. Li frames the issue as a division of labor rather than a rejection of internal evaluation: developers&\#x27; own research can still assess individual models, but it should be complemented by government, industry, and academic oversight. The report is brief and does not specify concrete mechanisms, such as who would certify evaluators, what standards would apply, or how such oversight would be enforced.

aibase · AIbase · Sep 23, 12:01

**Background**: Fei-Fei Li is a Stanford professor and one of the most influential figures in computer vision and modern AI, known for creating the ImageNet dataset that helped trigger the deep learning boom. AI safety evaluation generally covers risks such as misuse, bias, deception, and loss of human control, and current practice relies heavily on the labs themselves to test and disclose the behavior of their own models before release. Critics argue this creates a conflict of interest, since companies have commercial incentives to downplay weaknesses, which is why proposals for independent auditors and standardized third-party testing have gained traction in policy discussions worldwide.
