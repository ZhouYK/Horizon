## [France&\#x27;s 10-year borrowing costs hit 5% for the first time since 2002](https://www.ft.com/content/76f076e4-9db2-4971-8ac9-73d9f39e4876?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

France&\#x27;s 10-year government bond yield has reached 5% for the first time since 2002, climbing from roughly 3.2% in late February as investors worried about the country&\#x27;s public finances and political stability. The move marks a sharp selloff in French sovereign debt over a matter of months rather than years. A 5% yield is a psychological and practical threshold: it raises the cost of servicing France&\#x27;s already large debt burden and could widen spreads against German Bunds, pressuring eurozone sovereigns, banks that hold French paper, and the euro itself. Because France is one of the euro area&\#x27;s core anchor economies, a repricing of its risk premium raises questions about the durability of the currency bloc&\#x27;s debt architecture. The scale of the move — roughly 180 basis points in a few months — reflects a repricing of both fiscal and political risk rather than a change in the European Central Bank&\#x27;s policy rate, since core eurozone yields have not risen uniformly. The key number to watch is the France–Germany spread, which measures how much extra yield investors demand to hold French debt instead of Bunds.

rss · FT Markets · Oct 21, 16:33

**Background**: Government bonds are IOUs issued by states; their yields move inversely to prices and represent the annual return investors demand to lend to that government over a given period, in this case 10 years. So when traders sell off French debt \(OATs\), the yield rises, signalling that markets see more risk in lending to France. Rising yields feed directly into the interest bill on new borrowing, which compounds quickly for a country with a debt stock of well over 100% of GDP and a deficit that has repeatedly exceeded EU limits. In the eurozone, the gap between a country&\#x27;s yield and Germany&\#x27;s Bund yield is the standard gauge of perceived sovereign risk, and widening spreads were at the heart of the 2010–2012 debt crisis.
