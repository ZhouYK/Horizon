## [Hormuz oil shipments fall as Iranian strikes escalate](https://www.ft.com/content/5fbc2b9d-0360-4a1f-9e4a-3e86168cbbc5?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Oil shipments transiting the Strait of Hormuz are falling as Iranian strikes increase, and analysts quoted by the Financial Times say the recovery in Middle Eastern crude exports now looks &quot;increasingly fragile.&quot; The report signals a reversal of the tentative rebound in Gulf export volumes seen earlier. The Strait of Hormuz is the world&\#x27;s most important oil chokepoint, so any sustained drop in flows there directly tightens global crude supply and feeds into oil prices, energy equities, freight and insurance costs, and ultimately broader inflation. Because much of the crude passing through it is destined for Asian refiners, the disruption is felt well beyond the Middle East. The available report excerpt does not disclose specific volume or price figures, so the scale of the decline has to be inferred from tanker-transit tracking and war-risk insurance premiums rather than confirmed export data. It is also worth noting that Qatar&\#x27;s liquefied natural gas exports leave the Gulf through the same strait, so a prolonged disruption would hit gas markets as well as oil.

rss · FT Markets · Nov 21, 05:00

**Background**: The Strait of Hormuz is a narrow waterway between Iran and Oman that connects the Gulf to the open ocean, and roughly a fifth of global oil consumption is shipped through it each day, mostly from Saudi Arabia, Iraq, the UAE, Kuwait and Iran itself. Because it is so narrow and lies right next to Iranian territory, it is the classic example of an energy &quot;chokepoint&quot; in which a relatively small military escalation can move global prices. Bypass options such as Saudi Arabia&\#x27;s East-West pipeline and the UAE&\#x27;s Fujairah link exist, but they carry only a fraction of normal Gulf volumes, which is why traders watch Hormuz transit numbers so closely.

## [Traders Shift US Copper to China as Trump Tariff Fears Ease](https://www.ft.com/content/b84dbcb1-f216-422e-89c7-f203e0ab2757?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Copper traders have started moving US-held copper into China to capture a lucrative arbitrage created by an acute Chinese shortage and high local premiums, as fears over Trump-era copper tariffs ease. The reversal highlights how tariff expectations, rather than supply and demand alone, have been reshaping global copper trade flows, and a sustained shift could tighten US inventories, ease China&\#x27;s shortage and move copper prices and copper-exposed stocks. The trade works by exploiting the spread between US copper prices, typically CME/COMEX contracts, and Chinese domestic prices plus import premiums, with shipping costs, logistics and renewed tariff risk determining whether the arbitrage stays profitable; the report gives no specific price, volume or company figures.

rss · FT Markets · Nov 21, 12:00

**Background**: Copper is one of the most heavily traded industrial metals, and its prices are set on several exchanges, notably the London Metal Exchange \(LME\) in Europe, CME/COMEX in the US and the Shanghai Futures Exchange \(SHFE\) in China. Because these markets are separated by geography, freight costs, import duties and local supply conditions, price gaps open up that traders can arbitrage by buying in one market and selling in another. China is the world&\#x27;s largest copper consumer, so any acute domestic shortage tends to push Chinese prices and import premiums above overseas levels, pulling metal toward China. Tariff threats, such as those floated by the Trump administration on copper imports, can distort these flows by making traders reluctant to move metal into or out of the US.
