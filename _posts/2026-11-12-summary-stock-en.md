## [SEC Enforcement Against Public Companies Falls to 16-Year Low](https://www.ft.com/content/74fa38d2-6925-4204-a61b-192e5277e063?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 6.0/10

According to a report cited by the Financial Times, the US Securities and Exchange Commission brought the fewest enforcement actions against public companies since at least 2010, marking a 16-year low. The drop represents a notable pullback in regulatory scrutiny of corporate conduct by Wall Street&\#x27;s main watchdog. Fewer enforcement actions can weaken deterrence, potentially reducing the perceived cost of accounting fraud, disclosure failures, and other securities-law violations for issuers and their executives. The trend matters for investors who rely on regulatory oversight as a check on corporate accountability, and it may signal a broader shift in how aggressively the SEC polices public companies. The figure covers actions against corporations specifically, distinct from the SEC&\#x27;s broader case count that includes individuals, and the comparison point is at least 2010, meaning the current total is the lowest in the roughly 16 years of available data. The report frames the decline as a structural shift in enforcement priorities rather than a one-off dip.

rss · FT Markets · Nov 12, 11:00

**Background**: The SEC is the US federal agency responsible for enforcing securities laws and protecting investors in public markets. Its enforcement division pursues cases against companies and executives over issues such as financial misstatements, insider trading, bribery under the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, and failures to disclose material information. Enforcement actions can take the form of civil lawsuits in federal court or in-house administrative proceedings, and typically result in penalties, disgorgement of profits, injunctions, or settlements. The annual volume of such cases is widely watched as a gauge of how actively regulators are policing corporate behavior.

Calls are mounting in Washington for legislation to overhaul the US permitting system, which is widely seen as slow and costly, in order to speed construction of oil and gas infrastructure. The push comes as the Trump administration pursues a fossil fuel agenda and as overseas conflicts sharpen concerns about energy security. Permitting is one of the few areas where a legislative change could materially shorten the years-long timelines for pipelines, export terminals and transmission, so any deal would directly affect energy producers, midstream companies, utilities and consumers. It also sits at the intersection of the administration&\#x27;s fossil fuel priorities and bipartisan energy security concerns, making it one of the more plausible pieces of energy legislation to advance. **Background**: In the US, large energy projects must clear a tangle of federal authorizations: environmental reviews under the National Environmental Policy Act \(NEPA\), permits under the Clean Water Act and Clean Air Act, plus Federal Energy Regulatory Commission \(FERC\) approval for interstate pipelines. ## [China&\#x27;s securities regulator signals market resilience as long-term funds net-buy over 600 billion yuan of A-shares](https://finance.caixin.com/2026-11-12/102504124.html) ⭐️ 6.0/10

Speaking at the 2026 Shanghai Stock Exchange International Investor Conference, China Securities Regulatory Commission Vice Chairman Liu Haoling said China&\#x27;s development offers long-term certainty and that its capital markets have demonstrated resilience, with industrial innovation value being rapidly unlocked. He disclosed that long-term funds — including social security funds, enterprise annuities and insurance capital — have net-bought more than 600 billion yuan of A-shares since the start of the year, with both scale and allocation ratios rising steadily. The figures give official confirmation that long-term institutional money is becoming a structural source of demand for A-shares, which supports market liquidity and sentiment at a time of global liquidity swings and geopolitical friction. The endorsement reinforces Beijing&\#x27;s &quot;long money, long investment&quot; policy direction, which is intended to reduce the market&\#x27;s reliance on retail and short-term speculative flows. The remarks were delivered at a high-profile international investor conference hosted by the Shanghai Stock Exchange and were published via the exchange&\#x27;s official WeChat account. The resilience claim is anchored on macro data — GDP exceeding 107 trillion yuan in the first three quarters, up 4.6% year on year — but the disclosure largely summarises an existing trend rather than announcing new policy measures.

rss · 财新网 · Nov 12, 11:08

**Background**: &quot;Long money, long investment&quot; \(长钱长投\) is a policy concept in China that refers to steering capital with long horizons — insurance funds, the social security fund, basic pension funds, enterprise and occupational annuities, and mutual funds — into longer-term equity holdings; such money typically has investment cycles of five to ten years or more. In January 2025, five government departments jointly issued an implementation plan for promoting medium- and long-term capital into the market, a follow-up to a series of measures including the 2023 adjustment by the Ministry of Finance that set the social security fund&\#x27;s maximum equity allocation at 40%. The Shanghai Stock Exchange International Investor Conference is an annual event used by regulators to communicate with overseas and domestic institutional investors.
