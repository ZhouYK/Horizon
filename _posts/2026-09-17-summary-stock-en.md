## [US Regulator Opens Markets to Tokenized Stock Trading](https://www.ft.com/content/b528698e-9b04-4497-9d22-67e401be21d3?syn-25a6b1a6=1) ⭐️ 9.0/10

A US regulator has formally opened the door to tokenized stock trading, allowing blockchain-based representations of traditional equities to trade around the clock and to be used more easily as collateral. The ruling, reported by the Financial Times, marks the first time a US securities regulator has given such explicit latitude to on-chain equity products. The decision could materially reshape market structure: tokenized equities blur the line between crypto and traditional finance by offering 24/7 trading and collateral utility that conventional exchanges and brokers cannot match. Tokenization platforms and crypto-native venues stand to benefit, while incumbent exchanges and brokers face pressure to adapt their hours, settlement and custody models. Tokenized equities are typically 1:1 asset-backed, meaning each token is backed by a corresponding share held by a custodian, and they trade continuously rather than during fixed exchange hours. Important caveats remain: availability is often restricted for US retail users and certain prohibited jurisdictions, and collateral use typically happens in isolated on-chain lending markets or against perpetual futures rather than in mainstream brokerage margin accounts.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 17, 18:29

**Background**: Tokenization means issuing a blockchain token that represents an off-chain asset, in this case a listed company&\#x27;s share, usually held 1:1 by a custodian so the token tracks the share price. Traditional US equities trade only during exchange hours and settle on a T+1 basis, and shares used as collateral move slowly through custodians and prime brokers. Tokenized versions remove those frictions, letting holders trade at any hour and pledge assets in DeFi lending or derivatives markets. Platforms such as xStocks, Ondo Finance, Robinhood and Coinbase have already built tokenized equity products, and a clear US regulatory stance is the missing piece for broader institutional adoption.

## [Bank of England Holds Rates, Signals Tightening and a 2034 QT Endpoint](https://www.ft.com/content/cc14705c-22a9-431c-a98d-b9577be8aab5) ⭐️ 9.0/10

At its September rate decision, the Bank of England left its policy rate unchanged but signaled a tightening bias ahead. It also announced a multiyear plan to fully unwind its Asset Purchase Facility \(APF\), selling off the gilt holdings accumulated during the quantitative-easing era by 2034. A central bank that pairs a rate hold with an explicit tightening bias and a formal balance-sheet runoff schedule shapes expectations for gilt yields, sterling and global interest-rate pricing. Committing to a full unwind of QE-era purchases removes decades of central-bank demand for UK government debt, which affects borrowing costs for the government, banks and households. The unwind is framed as a multiyear effort stretching to 2034, indicating a gradual rather than abrupt pace of asset reduction. The APF holds financial assets such as government bonds that were bought to improve liquidity in credit markets, so the plan effectively schedules the end of the BoE&\#x27;s QE-era gilt portfolio.

rss · FT Markets · Sep 17, 11:29

**Background**: Quantitative easing \(QE\) is when a central bank creates money to buy assets, pushing up asset prices and stimulating the economy; quantitative tightening \(QT\) is the reverse, shrinking the balance sheet to drain liquidity and push rates higher. The Bank of England&\#x27;s Asset Purchase Facility is the vehicle that holds these purchases, largely UK government bonds known as gilts. QT is rare and has mainly been used after long periods of stimulus, as in 2008, 2018 and 2022.

The move sharpens the policy divergence between the two central banks, with the Fed raising rates while the BOJ has so far kept its ultra-loose stance. The widening gap between a tightening Fed and a still-accommodative BOJ pushes the yen lower and can ripple through global currency and asset markets, since yen weakness affects Japanese import costs, corporate earnings and carry-trade flows. The yen&\#x27;s slide also matters because it raises the cost of imported energy and food, feeding into domestic inflation.

The Federal Reserve has been raising rates to fight inflation, while the Bank of Japan has kept borrowing costs ultra-low for years to revive stagnant growth and inflation. This divergence is what has been pressuring the yen, a dynamic currency traders watch closely because Japan&\#x27;s policies can influence funding costs worldwide.

The new bill represents a shift toward so-called secondary measures, which penalize the buyers rather than the seller.
