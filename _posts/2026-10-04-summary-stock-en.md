On January 3, 2026, the United States launched a surprise operation in Venezuela and announced it had taken control of President Nicolás Maduro and his wife, a move widely interpreted as aimed at expanding U.S. energy interests in the country holding the world&\#x27;s largest crude oil reserves. Earlier, on December 4, 2025, the Democratic Republic of the Congo signed a strategic partnership agreement with the United States, following a February letter from Congolese President Félix Tshisekedi offering critical minerals in exchange for security guarantees and military support. The combination of rising resource nationalism and direct U.S. strategic moves in Venezuela and the DRC elevates critical minerals from a commercial commodity story to a core arena of great-power competition, potentially reshaping global supply chains for oil, copper and cobalt. Venezuela holds the world&\#x27;s largest proven crude oil reserves, while the DRC is central to global cobalt and copper supply — the TFM copper-cobalt mine, in which China&\#x27;s CMOC holds an 80% interest, is one of the world&\#x27;s largest and highest-grade producing copper-cobalt mines. The DRC deal is explicitly framed as minerals-for-security, meaning geopolitical alignment rather than price alone may determine who gets access to these resources.

**Background**: Critical minerals such as cobalt and copper are essential inputs for lithium-ion batteries, power grids and renewable energy systems, making their supply a matter of national security for major economies. As the global economy shifts toward more energy- and material-intensive models — electrification, data centers and defense — demand for these minerals has surged, prompting producer countries to assert greater control over their resources, a trend known as resource nationalism. The United States and China are both racing to lock in access to overseas mines, and African and Latin American producers are increasingly trading resources for security, investment or diplomatic backing.
